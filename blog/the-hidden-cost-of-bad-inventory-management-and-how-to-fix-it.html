The Hidden Cost of Bad Inventory Management (And How to Fix It)

By Scott Nguyen · Published May 21, 2025 · Updated 2026-09-27 · 3 min read

Inventory problems rarely show up as one clean line on a profit-and-loss statement. Stockouts create missed sales and account disruption; excess inventory ties up cash and can increase storage, markdown and obsolescence risk. The fix starts with a repeatable replenishment process rather than ordering when someone notices stock is low.

1. Separate sellable inventory from inventory that is only expected

Track what is available now, what is committed to open orders, what is in transit, what is being produced and what is not sellable. Treating all of those quantities as “inventory” can make the business look better stocked than it really is.

2. Build demand history at SKU and channel level

Use actual sales history, but flag periods affected by stockouts, major promotions, launches or one-time wholesale orders. A simple average can be misleading when the history includes days the item could not be sold.

3. Measure realistic replenishment lead time

Lead time is more than factory production. Include purchase-order approval, manufacturing, freight, customs where relevant, receiving and the time required before inventory is actually sellable. Track variability, not only the supplier’s quoted best case.

4. Set a replenishment trigger

A practical reorder trigger considers expected demand during lead time, open purchase orders, committed stock and a safety-stock assumption. The right buffer depends on demand variability, supplier reliability, service expectations and the cost of holding inventory.

5. Allocate inventory across channels deliberately

Amazon FBA, Walmart WFS, DTC, retail customers and 3PL inventory all compete for the same physical stock. Build channel allocations around demand and replenishment rather than allowing the loudest channel to consume every available unit.

6. Quantify the cost of excess inventory

Look at cash tied up, storage, financing, expiry or obsolescence risk and markdown exposure. An item can still be profitable on paper while creating a working-capital problem because too much inventory was purchased too early.

7. Review exceptions instead of blindly following the forecast

Forecasts are inputs, not facts. Review large demand changes, supplier delays, new promotions and channel launches as exceptions, then document why the replenishment plan changed.

8. Build a weekly inventory exception view

Instead of reviewing every SKU with the same attention, flag products that are projected to stock out before the next receipt, products with unusually high weeks of supply, delayed purchase orders, inventory discrepancies and items affected by upcoming promotions. That turns inventory planning into a manageable operating queue.

9. Connect inventory to cash

Translate excess units into dollars at landed cost and compare that cash with the next purchase orders the business needs to fund. This makes overstock visible as a working-capital decision rather than only a storage problem.

10. Measure forecast error where it changes decisions

Do not chase perfect forecasts. Track whether the error is large enough to change purchase quantities, stockout risk or excess inventory. Focus analysis on high-value, high-volume or volatile SKUs where a better decision materially affects the business.

11. Review inventory with a consistent cadence

Set a weekly or biweekly review for the SKUs that matter most rather than waiting for a crisis. Compare sellable stock, open purchase orders, inbound timing, recent demand, upcoming promotions and any channel-specific allocations. Record the reason for major purchase-order changes so later forecast reviews can distinguish a demand error from a deliberate business decision.
