How to Choose the Right 3PL — And What to Look for in the Contract

A 3PL should be evaluated as an operating partner, not just a warehouse price list. The best provider for one brand can be a poor fit for another because product profile, channels, parcel mix, retailer requirements, systems and exception volume all change the workload.

1. Write the requirements before asking for quotes

Document monthly orders, peak volume, SKU count, units per order, case and pallet receipts, product dimensions, special handling, dangerous-goods status if relevant, marketplaces, retail accounts, return volume and shipping destinations. Give every provider the same scenario so quotes can actually be compared.

2. Compare the entire fee structure

Look beyond pick-and-pack. Include receiving, pallet or bin storage, carton handling, packaging materials, parcel rates, return processing, account-management fees, kitting, labelling, special projects, EDI or integration costs, minimums and onboarding charges. Model a normal month and a peak month.

3. Define inventory-control expectations

Ask how receipts are verified, discrepancies are reported, cycle counts are handled, damaged goods are segregated and adjustments are approved. Decide what inventory reports you need and how your internal records will be reconciled against the warehouse.

4. Check channel-specific capabilities

If you sell through Amazon, Walmart, Best Buy or other retailers, document the exact inbound, labelling, ASN, appointment or parcel requirements the 3PL may need to support. “We work with marketplaces” is not enough; ask how the process works and what evidence you receive when an exception occurs.

5. Make service levels measurable

Define order cutoff, same-day or next-day expectations, receiving turnaround, inventory accuracy, response time, return processing and escalation. The contract should say how performance is measured and what happens when repeated failures occur.

6. Review liability, claims and exit terms

Understand insurance responsibilities, inventory-loss treatment, claims documentation, limitations of liability, lien rights, termination notice, data export and the process for removing inventory. Have legal counsel review contract language where appropriate.

7. Plan onboarding as a project

Set owners for inventory transfer, systems, SKU mapping, packaging rules, shipping accounts, marketplace integrations, test orders and reconciliation. Do not move the full operation until the critical data and process handoffs have been tested.

If you want an independent operating review before selecting or onboarding a warehouse, see 3PL and fulfilment consulting.

8. Test the provider with real scenarios

Ask each finalist how it would handle a normal DTC order, a marketplace prep shipment, a damaged return, a receiving shortage, a peak-day surge and an inventory mismatch. The answers reveal more than a generic capability deck because they show who owns the exception, what evidence is produced and how quickly the issue is escalated.

9. Build a comparison scorecard

Score providers using the criteria that matter to your business: total cost, geographic coverage, systems, retail or marketplace capability, inventory controls, returns, service levels, reporting, implementation support and exit flexibility. Weight the criteria before seeing the final proposals so the cheapest quote does not automatically become the decision.

10. Protect the transition

Keep enough overlap or contingency inventory to absorb onboarding issues where practical. Reconcile opening inventory, validate test orders, confirm tracking and returns, and document who signs off before the new warehouse becomes the sole operating location.
