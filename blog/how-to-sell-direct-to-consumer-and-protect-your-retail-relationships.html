How to Sell Direct-to-Consumer and Protect Your Retail Relationships

By Scott Nguyen · Published Jun 12, 2024 · Updated 2026-09-28 · 3 min read

Direct-to-consumer (DTC) sales offer better margins, richer customer data, and more control over your brand experience. But for brands that also sell through retailers, launching a DTC channel requires careful management of those retail relationships.

Have the Conversation Before You Launch

The worst way a retail partner learns about your DTC channel is seeing it in the market. If you have significant retail relationships, proactively communicate your DTC plans before launch. Frame it as brand building that will drive awareness and traffic to their stores, not competition.

Maintain Retail Price Integrity

The most common point of conflict between DTC and retail channels is pricing. If your DTC price is lower than the retail price, you're competing directly with your partners. Set your DTC price at or above the retail price and compete on selection, exclusives, and experience rather than price.

Offer DTC-Exclusive Products

Create products, bundles, or configurations that are only available DTC. This gives loyal customers a reason to buy from you directly while giving retail partners assortment that isn't available elsewhere. The exclusive DTC range doesn't compete with retail because it can't be purchased there.

Use DTC Data to Strengthen Retail Performance

Aggregated DTC information can help explain geographic demand or reorder behaviour. Use only data you can support and share appropriately; label hypothetical examples clearly.

The fix: Before launching or expanding your DTC channel, review your retail agreements for any exclusivity or pricing clauses that might be affected. Address any conflicts proactively.

Define the role of DTC in the channel strategy

Decide whether the store exists for full assortment, education, new launches, subscriptions, geographic coverage or another purpose. That makes pricing and promotion decisions easier to coordinate with retail partners.

Use product and offer differentiation deliberately

Bundles, exclusive colours, subscriptions or value-added content can give DTC a reason to exist without making every retailer compete against an identical offer. Confirm that any exclusivity or pricing commitments are respected.

Coordinate promotions and inventory

Keep a shared calendar of major discounts, launches and retail events. Review channel inventory before promotions so DTC demand does not create retail stockouts or breach supply commitments.

Measure the channels on contribution and strategic value

DTC may provide richer customer data while retail provides reach and volume. Compare contribution, acquisition cost, repeat behaviour, returns and working capital rather than forcing every channel to perform the same role.

For cross-channel planning, see fractional e-commerce management.

Set internal rules for channel exceptions

Define who can approve DTC discounts, bundles or exclusives that may affect retailers. Keep a record of exceptions and the business reason so short-term promotions do not accidentally undermine longer-term account strategy.

Use DTC insights to support retail

Customer questions, product reviews and repeat-purchase data from your own store can improve retailer content, assortment and forecasts. Share useful insights internally without exposing customer data inappropriately.

Create a shared promotion calendar

Record major DTC and retailer promotions, product launches and exclusive periods. Give account teams enough lead time to identify conflicts before campaigns go live.

Review channel feedback together

Retail sell-through, DTC search behaviour and customer-service questions can inform assortment decisions across channels. Avoid managing each channel in isolation when the same products and inventory are shared.
