Google Ads vs Facebook Ads: Which Is Right for Your Business?

The Google vs Facebook debate is one of the most common questions I get from e-commerce and service businesses. The honest answer is that both work — but for fundamentally different reasons.

Google Captures Demand. Facebook Creates It.

When someone searches Google for "e-commerce consultant Ontario," they're actively looking to solve a problem. Google puts your solution in front of them at the exact moment of intent. Facebook users aren't searching for anything — you're interrupting their scroll and trying to create interest that didn't exist yet.

When Google Wins

Google is stronger for high-intent purchases, service businesses, products with established search demand, and B2B. If people are actively searching for what you sell, Google captures that demand efficiently.

When Facebook Wins

Facebook is stronger for new product launches, impulse purchases, broad audience reach, and building brand awareness. If you're creating a new category or selling something buyers don't know to search for yet, Facebook builds the awareness that eventually drives Google searches.

The Real Answer for Most Businesses

Use both, but start with where your buyers are. For most service businesses and established product categories, start with Google. For new products, lifestyle brands, and visual products, start with Facebook. Once profitable on one, expand to the other.

The fix: Define where your buyer is in the purchase journey when they first encounter you. If they're searching, start with Google. If they don't know they need you yet, start with Facebook.

Compare intent, not platform popularity

Google Search can reach people actively expressing a need, while Meta platforms can help create or capture demand through audience and creative signals. The stronger starting point depends on how customers discover the product or service.

Use the same economic framework

Compare qualified acquisition cost, contribution, close rate, repeat value and creative workload rather than CPC or CPM alone. Each platform attributes conversions differently, so reconcile with business records.

Consider the strength of the creative asset base

Meta often depends heavily on creative testing, while Google Search depends more directly on query, offer and landing-page fit. A business with no strong visual content may face a different setup burden than one with an established content engine.

Test one clear hypothesis at a time

Choose the platform that best matches the immediate customer journey, run a controlled test and expand when the economics are understood.

Compare creative and operating burden

Meta may require a steady flow of fresh visual creative, while Google Search requires disciplined query, ad and landing-page management. Include the internal time and production capacity needed to keep each channel healthy.

Use a controlled budget split when both make sense

Define what each platform is meant to prove and keep attribution differences in mind. Compare qualified acquisition and contribution over the same business period rather than declaring a winner from platform-reported conversions alone.

Compare assisted roles

One platform may create awareness while another captures the final search. Review cross-channel customer journeys before cutting a channel that appears weaker on last-click attribution.

Use creative and query learning across channels

Customer language from Google Search can inform Meta creative, while high-performing Meta concepts can inspire landing-page messaging. Treat the channels as sources of customer insight as well as media.
