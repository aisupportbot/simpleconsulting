How to Build SOPs That Your Team Actually Follows

Standard Operating Procedures are the backbone of a scalable business. Most businesses know they need them. Very few have ones that actually get followed. Here's the difference.

Why SOPs Fail

They're too long. They're written in passive voice. They assume knowledge the reader doesn't have. They were written by someone who no longer does the task. They live in a folder nobody opens.

The Format That Gets Followed

The best SOPs are short, specific, and visual. Lead with the outcome — what does "done correctly" look like? Then list the steps in numbered sequence. Include screenshots for digital tasks. Include photos for physical tasks. If a step requires a decision, include a decision tree — not a paragraph of explanation.

Build SOPs With the People Who Do the Work

The best SOP is written by the person who performs the task, reviewed by the person who manages it, and tested by someone who has never done it. If the new person can complete the task correctly using only the SOP, it works. If they can't, it needs revision.

Where to Store Them

SOPs only work if people can find them when they need them. Google Drive with a clear folder structure, Notion, or a dedicated wiki all work well. The location matters less than the consistency. Pick one place and put everything there.

The fix: Pick your single most error-prone process. Write the SOP today, test it with one team member, and refine it based on where they got stuck.

Write the SOP around a specific outcome

State what triggers the process, what a completed result looks like and which system or record proves completion. Avoid turning the SOP into a long policy document that does not help someone execute the task.

Use the level of detail the risk requires

High-risk tasks may need screenshots, approval points or exception rules; routine tasks may only need a checklist. Include examples where judgement is required.

Test the SOP with someone who did not write it

Ask a trained team member to follow the document without verbal coaching. Record where they hesitate or need missing information, then revise the instructions.

Assign ownership and review dates

Every SOP should have an owner responsible for updating it when systems, marketplace rules or team responsibilities change. Archive outdated versions so employees do not follow conflicting instructions.

Design exception paths explicitly

Most SOPs work until something unusual happens. Add common exceptions, escalation criteria and examples of when the operator should stop and ask for help rather than forcing the standard process.

Link the SOP to the system of record

Tell the user where completion is documented and which fields or evidence prove the task is done. This turns the SOP from a reading document into part of the operating control.

Use version control

Include owner, last updated date and version. Archive old copies and link employees to one current location so outdated PDFs do not continue circulating.

Measure adherence selectively

For critical processes, audit a small sample of completed work against the SOP. Use misses to improve training or the document rather than assuming non-compliance is always an employee problem.
