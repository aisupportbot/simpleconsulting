How to Turn One-Time Clients Into Long-Term Retainer Relationships

By Scott Nguyen · Oct 9, 2024 · 1 min read

Project-based consulting work is unpredictable by nature. One month you're turning away work, the next you're scrambling for new clients. Retainer relationships solve this — but getting clients to agree to them requires the right approach.

Deliver Value in the Project That Makes the Retainer Obvious

The best time to introduce a retainer is after you've delivered results in a project. "I've fixed X problem. But without ongoing attention to Y and Z, the problem will likely return. Here's what a monthly engagement to maintain and build on this would look like." The retainer should feel like the natural next step, not an upsell.

Define the Retainer Scope Clearly

Vague retainers cause friction. "Ongoing consulting support" is too vague. "Monthly strategy review, weekly check-in, unlimited email support, and one deliverable per month" is specific enough that both parties know what's included. Clarity reduces the ambiguity that kills retainer relationships.

Price for Ongoing Value, Not Hours

Retainer pricing based on hours is a trap. It creates an adversarial relationship where the client tries to get maximum hours and you try to minimize scope. Price based on the value you're delivering: what is the outcome of this ongoing work worth to the client's business?

Build in a Review Cadence

Schedule a formal quarterly review of the retainer relationship. What did we accomplish? What's next? Is this still the right scope? This prevents retainers from becoming stale and gives both parties a structured opportunity to adjust rather than waiting for frustration to accumulate.

The fix: Identify your last three completed projects. For which clients would an ongoing engagement make sense? Reach out with a specific retainer proposal within the next two weeks.
