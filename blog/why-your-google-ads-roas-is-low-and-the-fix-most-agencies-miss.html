Why Your Google Ads ROAS Is Low — And the Fix Most Agencies Miss

A low ROAS on Google Ads is one of the most common complaints I hear from businesses who've tried working with agencies. The real problem is usually neither the budget nor the agency — it's the fundamentals.

Check Your Search Term Reports First

Most agencies set up campaigns and let them run. What they don't do is dig into the search term report. Without aggressive negative keyword lists, you're paying for irrelevant traffic that will never convert.

Your Landing Page Is the Real Problem

They optimize the ad. They ignore the page it sends traffic to. If your landing page is slow or doesn't match the ad's promise, your Quality Score drops, CPCs rise, and conversion rate tanks.

Attribution Is Lying to You

Last-click attribution makes Google Ads look worse than it is for upper-funnel campaigns. Switch to data-driven attribution and look at assisted conversions before making budget decisions.

The fix: Pull your search term report. Add negatives aggressively. Load your landing page on mobile and time it. Fix those two things before touching your bids.

Confirm the revenue and attribution basis first

Make sure conversion values, currencies, refunds and attribution settings are understood before diagnosing ROAS. A tracking problem can look like a campaign problem.

Separate traffic quality from offer quality

Search terms, audiences and placements may be relevant while the landing page, product price or stock position is weak. Diagnose the full path before cutting bids.

Look at product or service contribution

ROAS does not include cost of goods, fulfilment, returns or sales labour. A lower-ROAS product with strong margin can sometimes create more profit than a high-ROAS low-margin offer.

Review marginal spend

Ask what the next dollar of budget is producing, not only the historical average. Campaigns can look efficient overall while recent incremental spend has become less productive.

Build a ROAS bridge

Break the change into traffic cost, conversion rate, average order value or lead value, product mix and tracking changes. This shows which component actually drove the decline instead of treating ROAS as one opaque number.

Compare blended and incremental economics

Look at the overall account, then isolate the campaigns or products receiving additional spend. Strong historical ROAS can hide weak returns on the newest budget being added.

Check product and customer mix

A shift toward lower-priced products, new customers or more competitive categories can lower ROAS even when campaign execution is stable. Compare mix before assuming optimization failed.

Review lag and attribution timing

Some conversions arrive days after the click. Use a reporting window appropriate to the buying cycle before reacting to very recent spend.

Test whether the account is budget-constrained or demand-constrained

If strong campaigns are limited by budget, reallocating spend may help. If demand itself is weak, increasing budget can simply buy more expensive traffic.

Bring finance into the review

Use contribution and margin data to determine what ROAS is actually acceptable by product or service. One universal target can misallocate spend across very different economics.
